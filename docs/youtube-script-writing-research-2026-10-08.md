# YouTube script-writing research — 8 October 2026

Scope: Soft Reset With Me's emotional relationship Shorts and approximately 2–3 minute horizontal videos. Research and editorial recommendations only: no prompts, model settings, or production files were changed. The user is editing those separately.

## Bottom line

Keep the concrete, fair, single-thesis approach. Improve the **sequence of meaningful changes**, not the number of hooks or the amount of drama. An original situation, a believable consequence, a genuinely changed interpretation, and a fulfilled opening promise are more useful than a succession of quotable slogans.

No source reviewed establishes a universal ideal duration, voice pitch, sentence length, cut frequency, or guaranteed viral formula for this audience. Timing suggestions below are editorial test hypotheses, not scientific thresholds.

## Platform evidence: what YouTube actually says

### Immediate hook, but not compulsory shock

YouTube's 28 January 2025 interview summary features Jenny Hoyos advocating a first-second hook and concise mini-stories. This is experienced **practitioner advice**, published by YouTube—not a controlled trial, a ranking specification, or a requirement that every video shock the audience. For this channel, recognition can create intrigue without threats or exaggeration. [Official Shorts deep dive](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/)

### Retention is a diagnosis, not a magic benchmark

YouTube recommends matching the opening to the title/thumbnail promise and experimenting with introductions. Its retention report defines the intro at 30 seconds, identifies dips and top moments, and warns that a rewatch spike can indicate confusion rather than enjoyment. That 30-second reporting boundary is not a recommendation to spend 30 seconds setting up a Short. [Audience retention help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)

YouTube advises choosing a length appropriate to the content, considering both relative and absolute watch time. It does not require daily uploads. Accordingly, 25–40 second Shorts and 2–3 minute long videos can be useful production starting points, not lengths proven best for US 18–35 relationship viewers. [Performance FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/141805)

### Current monetization wording matters more than old AI headlines

The current policy page explicitly addresses generic AI templates, interchangeable storylines, and emotionally manipulative formulas. It permits automation that still delivers an original creative perspective and satisfying narrative. Materially varied substance matters; changing stock clips while repeating the same story and outcome is insufficient protection. Reused-content review is separate from copyright: permission to use footage does not itself establish monetization eligibility. This describes wording retrieved on 8 October 2026; it does not infer an effective date for every present section. [Current channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392)

**Implication for our channel:** editorial intensity is fine; fabricating motives, repeatedly manufacturing distress, or withholding a trivial answer behind a misleading promise is the wrong direction. Monetization cannot be guaranteed by a prompt, a paid model, or licensed footage.

## Original research: useful principles and limits

**Narrative coherence contributes to attention.** A 2018 experiment assigned 80 psychology students to original or shuffled versions of two films, watching 40 minutes and responding to periodic tones. Intact narrative order was associated with greater attentional engagement as measured through the secondary task; local audiovisual features also mattered. Results varied by film, and this is not evidence that faster cuts are always worse or that any particular Short structure is optimal. [Original study: Does narrative drive dynamic attention to a prolonged stimulus?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6286290/)

**Engagement changes across a story.** A 2021 study examined a 20-minute audio narrative and a 50-minute television episode, combining behavioral ratings with independent fMRI datasets. Engagement tracked emotional content and narrative events, with engagement-related brain measures predicting later event recall. These longer lab narratives do not establish a feed-viewer attention span or prove that fear and negativity outperform gentler emotion. [Original study: Neural signatures of attentional engagement during narratives and its consequences for event memory](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8379980/)

**Our editorial inference:** make the viewer care about what a choice means, then change that meaning as the story unfolds. Do not interpret neuroscience as a license to manipulate vulnerable viewers or insert dopamine/oxytocin claims into the script.

## What to keep, reconsider, and add in the current Short prompt

These are recommendations from reading `prompts/script_prompt.txt`, not changes already implemented.

| Current instruction | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Observable opening, one thesis, fairness, calibrated motives | Keep | Clear identity; avoids generic or accusatory advice. |
| Every sentence must advance meaning | Keep, evaluate aloud | Meaning can advance through an action, consequence, contrast, or changed understanding. |
| “If you … this is for you” as a strong hook option | Demote to occasional use | It spends time announcing relevance; a concrete action can demonstrate it immediately. Editorial judgment, not platform law. |
| Every sentence under 12 words | Relax to a readability preference | A rigid limit can produce choppy, equal-weight sentences. No reviewed evidence supports that threshold. |
| “2–3 sharp points” in insight | Usually prefer one decisive distinction | Multiple advice points can turn the mini-story into a list halfway through. |
| Same five-step movement every time | Use as a quality check, not mandatory visible mold | Vary whether the story begins with a choice, consequence, dialogue, contradiction, or discovery. |
| Famous-sounding slogan examples | Use cautiously | Their confident absolutes can conflict with the prompt's own fairness rules; imitation can make scripts interchangeable. |
| Three meaningful data points sufficient for feedback | Treat as a minimum guard, not statistical reliability | Small, mismatched samples cannot prove a pattern. Account for format, topic, traffic source, publication age, and audience. |

### Candidate instructions to consider adding

- Open directly on one recognizable action or contradiction. Do not announce that the video is relevant.
- Make the next beat change something: an action happens, a consequence appears, or the viewer's initial interpretation becomes incomplete.
- Show the emotional cost through a specific moment before explaining it.
- Give the viewer a useful distinction before the final line; the ending should crystallize it, not reveal the first substantive value.
- The landing must answer the opening without promising guaranteed relationship outcomes.
- Vary sentence length and emphasis naturally. A quiet voice can still move from curiosity to tension to relief.
- Use recent scripts only to detect repeated premise, argument, imagery, and outcome—not merely duplicate wording.
- Ask whether every claimed action can be represented coherently with the available clip-only visual vocabulary.

## Format-specific working hypotheses

### The first three seconds

The following is a practical editorial heuristic, **not a proven three-second psychological threshold**:

- **0–1 second:** start recognition immediately through a concrete action, object, or situation. The first frame, readable caption, and voice should establish the same idea.
- **1–3 seconds:** introduce the meaningful contradiction or unanswered consequence that makes continuing worthwhile. Start a question in the viewer's mind; do not explain the whole pitch.
- Example: a message being composed, with the voice beginning “You type what hurt…” and the next beat “…then replace it with ‘I'm fine.’” The contradiction is honesty becoming concealment. An unrelated sad-person clip would establish mood but communicate that action less clearly.
- Avoid logo intros, greetings, “this video is for you,” a slow fade-in before the first words, and unsupported threats such as “If you do this, your relationship is doomed.”

YouTube's practitioner first-second advice concerns the **start of the hook**, not fitting a complete premise and resolution into one second. The current example hook takes longer than three seconds to speak calmly; that is a pacing consideration, not automatic failure.

### Short: one compact emotional incident

Start in the recognizable moment, show its consequence, then reveal one fair distinction that changes the meaning. End with the strongest precise formulation of that distinction. Avoid unrelated advice, three competing psychological explanations, and a second concluding paragraph that repeats the first.

For the test script: the typed message → deletion → thumbs-up is the strongest story sequence. “You feel strangely worse” is an opportunity to show the unresolved cost more specifically. “Edit the tone, not the truth” already resolves the thesis; any following line must add rather than restate.

### 2–3 minute video: depth, not an elongated Short

Use the opening scene to pose a question, then explore it with a second meaningful case or a counterexample. Introduce a distinction that survives both cases. Return to the first scene with a changed choice or interpretation. The second case should complicate the argument—not reproduce the same scene with another person.

An illustrative structure: honest-message scene → why deletion feels safer → cooling down is sometimes sensible → what gets lost when the need disappears → a clearer alternative → return to the original message. No invented statistics or clinical explanation is needed to make that argument.

## Small evaluation plan before accepting an edited prompt

1. Read one Short aloud and mark action, consequence, interpretation change, and payoff. Remove a sentence and ask what is lost.
2. Try the edited prompt on contrasting topics, not only the current message-deletion topic. Check whether the outputs retain their own premise and shape.
3. Review script and voice separately. Good text can flatten through uniform delivery; theatrical delivery cannot repair an empty middle.
4. Check opening visuals against the words and ensure a stock clip does not imply an actual identifiable person experienced the invented situation.
5. After publishing, inspect comparable videos over time. Use retention dips and comments as clues, not proof of causes; do not optimize solely for rewatches.

Priority: **progression first, natural spoken rhythm second, channel-level originality third**. More prompt length is not the objective.
